PAPA (theorem prover)

Linden says: theorems aren't gonna prove themselves! Use PAPA online here

This is the project page for PAPA, Peano Arithmetic Proof Assistant.

What is this?

PAPA is a proof assistant written in javascript and running in your browser. It is based on Peano arithmetic, with support for generics, defining new relations and functions on the fly (new functions can only be defined if you prove existence and uniqueness conditions); for example, relations <,≤,∣,≅<, \leq, |, \cong<,≤,∣,≅ are already defined in the "standard library", and have many results proven about them.

For any user-defined relation, you can click on it to expand it in the prover view.

There is direct support for the deduction theorem: you can make and discharge assumptions rather like natural deduction.

The current "most advanced" theorem is certainly the recursion theorem which I proved in mid 2025; since then I tried defining a programming language, which worked (open piano to see the definition); but proving anything about it was so tedious that I gave up. Maybe LLMs can help me, or maybe I just need better tools. As of today (June 2026) all theorems are hand-proved.

(More) formal specification

Definitions in bold; as far as I know they generally agree with the literature.

A term is something that boils down to a number, for example 0, (0)+(S(x)), 'Mod[x,y].

A formula is something that boils down to a true/false value, for example 0=S(0) (false), Ax((0)<=(x)) (true), (x)=(y) (sometimes true).

Each line is a statement, which is a pair (claim, assumptions). The claim is a formula and the assumptions are a finite set of formulas. For example, the statement (C, {A1, A2, A3}) is supposed to represent the idea that A1, A2, and A3 together imply C.

In maths textbooks this idea is usually written like A1,A2,A3⊢CA_1, A_2, A_3 \vdash CA1​,A2​,A3​⊢C.

In the theorem prover, the claim formula is always written out, but the assumptions are just referenced with line numbers (you can see a list of them on the far right of each line; a line may have no assumptions).

A statement with no assumptions and no free variables is called a theorem.

When you open up PAPA you get 14 statements to start you off. These never change and also don't have any assumptions. These 14 theorems are called the axioms of Peano arithmetic: 7 for general logic, and then 7 Peano axioms that describe what you can do with 0, SSS, + and ⋅\cdot⋅.
