Theorem proving IMO 2026 P6

Linden says: theorems aren't gonna prove themselves! Use PAPA online here

I recently had fun attempting this year's IMO questions with a group of friends. Collectively we managed all problems except P2P2P2 which was (horror) a geometry problem. Inability at geometry is basically expected given that few of us had thought about olympiad geometry in a long time, but it would have been nice to claim full marks. There's always next year!

This post is about P6P6P6, which was a fun number theory question about sequences. I thought the proof was elementary enough to fit under things I've already proved in PAPA, so I had a go, and a few days later I have (pretty much) formalised the whole argument by hand.

I say "pretty much" because the statement of the problem had to be somewhat changed so that the theorem prover can understand it. Here is the original problem statement:

IMO 2026 P6.

Let a1,a2,a3,...a_1, a_2, a_3, ...a1​,a2​,a3​,... be an infinite sequence of positive integers greater than 111. Prove that there exist positive integers TTT and LLL such that an+T=an+La_{n+T} = a_n + Lan+T​=an​+L for every positive integer nnn.

Firstly note that we have 1<a1<a2<...1 < a_1 < a_2 < ...1<a1​<a2​<..., so the sequence is effectively describing a subset of the positive integers.

It should be clear that a nonnegative integer nnn appearing in the list means either n=a1n=a_1n=a1​, or n>a1n>a_1n>a1​ and for every mmm in the sequence before nnn, we have that gcd(m,n)>1gcd(m,n)>1gcd(m,n)>1.

In fact this goes the other way as well: if n=a1n=a_1n=a1​ obviously nnn is in the sequence; alternatively if the second condition holds, there must be some aka_kak​ which is the largest element of the sequence less than nnn, at which point we must have ak+1=na_{k+1}=nak+1​=n from the definition.

Now write P[n]P[n]P[n] to denote the claim that nnn appears somewhere in the sequence. We've convinced ourselves of the following claim about PPP:

∀n,P[n]⇔(n=a1 or [n>a1 and ∀k<n,P[k]⇒gcd(n,k)>1])\forall n, P[n] \Lrarr (n=a_1 \text{ or } [n>a_1 \text{ and } \forall k < n, P[k] \Rarr gcd(n,k)>1])∀n,P[n]⇔(n=a1​ or [n>a1​ and ∀k<n,P[k]⇒gcd(n,k)>1])

This says that for any a1>1a_1>1a1​>1 and any predicate P[x]P[x]P[x] satisfying the above claim about PPP, we can conclude there exists a nonzero LLL such that:

All friendly numbers, and therefore all numbers in the sequence, are multiples of some novel numbers. This works backwards as well: any multiple of a novel number that's at least a1a_1a1​ will be in the sequence, because novel numbers are friendly.

So the sequence can be described as the set of numbers which are both at least a1a_1a1​ and also a multiple of some novel number. We will prove this later as lemma 6.

Our proof strategy will be to prove that there is only a finite number of novel numbers, and thus we can take LLL to be their product and have the result follow immediately. So let's suppose the following.

Assumption 1. There are infinitely many novel numbers.

inf[novel[P;a1];]

Define a supernovel number to be a novel number that introduces a new prime factor: that is, there is a prime dividing it which does not divide any smaller friendly number.

Lemma 2. There are infinitely many supernovel numbers.

This is because I didn't want to bother formally defining a function x↦pxx \mapsto p_xx↦px​ and also because division is sort of hard.
